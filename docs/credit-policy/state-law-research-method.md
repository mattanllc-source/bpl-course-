# How to research a new state

Training content, not legal advice. State counsel confirms every finding before a live deal.

Our state law reference covers four states. A deal in any other state needs its own research first. The method below is how you build that research. Your work goes to counsel; it does not replace counsel.

## Before you start

- **Use the law of the property's state.** Licensing, rate caps, foreclosure and liens follow where the property sits. Your company's home state does not decide them.
- **Read the statute itself.** Summaries from law firms, blogs and vendors can be out of date. Use them only to find the statute, then read the statute.
- **"Not found" is not "no rule".** If you cannot find a statute, write "Not found". Never write "none" because a search came up empty.

## The eight steps

Work through the steps in order. Step 3 covers doing business as an out-of-state lender. Step 6 covers mechanics' liens on construction and rehab loans.

### Step 1. Confirm the loan's purpose and collateral

Write down four facts before you search:

- Is the loan for a business purpose? Will the borrower or a relative live in the property?
- How many units? One to four units and five or more often follow different rules.
- Is the borrower an entity or a person?
- Will loan money pay for construction or repairs?

These facts pick the rows and rules you need. A rental house, a 40-unit apartment building and a strip center can each land in different parts of the same statute.

### Step 2. Find the licensing statute and the regulator's page

Look for two licenses: one for the lender and one for any broker. Start at the state's financial regulator. Its licensing pages usually name the statute and its exemptions. Then open the statute and read the exemption word for word.

Check whether the license turns on consumer purpose, on collateral type, or on any commercial loan. States differ on this.

### Step 3. Check whether your company must register to do business

Registering to do business with the Secretary of State is separate from a lending license. Most states let an out-of-state lender make, hold and enforce loans without registering. That safe harbor has limits. In North Carolina, it applies only if the lender keeps no office or agency in the state [VERIFY: G.S. 55-15-01(b)(6); 57D-7-01(b)(6); checked 24 Nov 2026].

Read three things in the state's business entity statute:

- The list of activities that are "not doing business". Look for making loans, taking mortgages and collecting debts.
- Whether the list differs by entity type. In Ohio, the limited liability company (LLC) statute lists lending and enforcing mortgages. We found no matching list for corporations [VERIFY: ORC 1706.512(A)(7), (8); ORC 1703.02; checked 24 Nov 2026].
- What happens if a company should have registered and did not. In Indiana, it may not maintain an action until it registers. Its contracts stay valid [VERIFY: IC 23-0.5-5-2(b), (c); checked 24 Nov 2026].

### Step 4. Find the rate, fee and prepayment rules

Find the usury statute that sets the rate cap first, then its exemptions. Exemptions often turn on loan size, an entity borrower, a business purpose or a licensed lender. Then look for limits on default interest and late fees, and on prepayment charges. Note any rule that applies only to owner-occupied homes.

### Step 5. Find the foreclosure, redemption and deficiency statutes

Answer four questions:

- **Method.** Does foreclosure go through the courts, or by a power of sale outside court?
- **Timeline.** What waiting periods and notices does the statute set?
- **Redemption.** Can the borrower buy the property back after the sale, and for how long? A long period delays your resale. In California, a judicial foreclosure with a deficiency judgment can carry a redemption period of up to one year [VERIFY: CCP 729.010(a), 729.030; checked 24 Nov 2026].
- **Deficiency.** If the sale does not cover the debt, can you sue the borrower or guarantor for the rest?

### Step 6. Find the mechanics' lien rules for construction or rehab loans

Contractors who are not paid can file liens on the property. Find out when a lien takes effect and whether it can rank ahead of your mortgage. In North Carolina, a lien relates back to the claimant's first delivery of labor or materials to the site [VERIFY: G.S. 44A-10; checked 24 Nov 2026]. So work that started before you recorded your mortgage can matter.

Also look for notices that protect lenders. Ohio uses a notice of commencement, and North Carolina uses a lien agent [VERIFY: ORC 1311.04(A); G.S. 44A-11.1(a); checked 24 Nov 2026].

### Step 7. Record the source, subsection and dates

For every answer, write down:

- the statute and its exact subsection, for example "(B)(1)", not just the section;
- the web address you opened;
- the version date the site shows, if any; and
- the date you checked it.

If the official site blocks you, use a well-known mirror and say so. Note the mirror's "current as of" date. Some mirrors run years behind.

### Step 8. Mark every answer and send it to counsel

Mark every answer as unverified until state counsel confirms it. List each open question for counsel in one sentence. Good questions name the statute and the exact doubt. Example: "Does a hearing before the clerk count as a court proceeding under G.S. 57D-7-02(a)?"

Do not issue a term sheet until counsel has answered.

## Common mistakes

- **Stopping at the license.** A license exemption says nothing about registering to do business, and the reverse is also true. Check both.
- **Reading one entity type.** Corporations and LLCs often have different statutes. Read the one for your company's entity type.
- **Trusting a summary's date.** A blog post from two years ago may predate an amendment. Open the statute and note its version date.
- **Missing the unit-count test.** Many protections apply to one or two units, or to owner-occupied homes only. Others reach all residential property. Read the definitions section.
- **Writing "none" for "not found".** Your search may have missed the rule. Say what you searched, and ask counsel.
